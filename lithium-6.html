The Lithium-6 Project

Fusion's fuel cycle has a missing ingredient. This project asked whether a crystal could fix it — and stopped at the bench.

The gap

Fusion runs on a metal nobody sells. Every deuterium-tritium fusion plant has to breed its own tritium by firing neutrons at lithium-6 — and no Western merchant supplier of enriched Li-6 is delivering at scale today. The old plants used mercury by the kiloton — more than eleven thousand tonnes of it at Oak Ridge alone — and closed decades ago. The depleted lithium-7 left over is scarce too: today's reactors want it for coolant chemistry, tomorrow's molten-salt reactors want tonnes of it. Two starved customers, one process. Australia digs up half the world's lithium; the interesting position is between the rock and the reactor.

The trick

A crystal that prefers the lighter atom. In 2025, the Banerjee lab published a mercury-free route: a tunnel-structured vanadium oxide (ζ-V₂O₅) whose channels bind ⁶Li over ⁷Li — a zero-point-energy effect, about 5.7% enrichment per electrochemical insertion step, in water, at a bench (Chem 2025). The same group published the full crystal synthesis recipe a decade earlier, open access (Chem. Sci. 2015). None of the science here is original — that's the point. Replicate first, then measure.

The chain

Li-6 sits upstream of more than fusion. Tritium is bred from lithium-6; helium-3 — the gas superconducting quantum computers can't get cold without — is what tritium decays into. ⁶Li → ³H → ³He. Supply the first link and you sit above two more starved markets without touching a reactor or a cryostat.

The experiment

The whole thesis fits on one borrowed bench. Make ~50 grams of the crystal, run it on commercial battery-grade lithium, and let a rented mass spectrometer referee. Four questions: can the crystal be made, does it separate on clean feedstock (the gate), does the residual silver stay locked in the lattice, and how many cycles does it survive. That last number decides whether the plant model closes at all — so it gets measured, not argued. Cost: roughly $11k in a partner lab. Full bill of materials, bench design, and the unit-economics model behind that margin figure exist and are available on request.

Open questions

Plenty, and they're written down. The method has been published once, at gram scale, by its inventors — outsider replication is the first real test. Fusion demand is mostly future demand; this could be early. Lithium-6 is dual-use, so the whole thing gets built in the open, safeguards first. And the field isn't empty: Hexium (laser enrichment, reviving LLNL's AVLIS playbook) and ASP Isotopes' Quantum Leap Energy (NASDAQ: ASPI) are circling the same market with heavier physics — read here as validation, not doom.

Where it stands

Parked, on purpose — September 2026. The desk work is done and the bench isn't: the crystal has not been made, and an honest run of the plant model over its own ranges found no corner that clears the cost bar at the price the lithium-7 market actually pays. So this stops here as a study. The thesis, the systems work and the open questions stand as written; the bench waits for a lab partner and a budget; and the next word on it will be a measurement, not another page. Anyone who has read the two papers and thinks this fails still gets the most valuable coffee.

On method

This project was researched and drafted in long collaboration with AI — literature, market maps, models, and most first drafts, including this page's. Every load-bearing claim traces to a cited paper or named source, not to model output, and the experiment above exists because nothing here is true until it's measured. The judgement calls and the responsibility sit with the author.
