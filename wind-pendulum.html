The wind pendulum

An experiment in giving an AI a way to detect and act in the world.

I put a fan on a stick, gave an LLM four numbers and one way to act, and asked it to model the world it was in.

How does it work? The setup is simple. A stick with a controllable fan is connected to the knob of a potentiometer. The agent can detect two things: the potentiometer reading, and the time. The agent has one way to act in the world: to turn on the fan at whichever speed, for any duration, and in either the clockwise or counter-clockwise direction.

The agent is dropped into the world with no context at all, and asked to simply model the world it exists in.

An interesting thing happens when this occurs. The agent may start by simply recording its observations, then modelling the world based on historical data.

Then it begins to make predictions, and test those predictions with its own experiments.

As you would imagine, it develops a fairly accurate model of the world it exists in through these means.

What happened

Fifty-two runs, over two sessions on one evening in September 2026, with the agent (Claude Code, in a sealed folder) choosing every run itself. It saw four numbers: fan power and direction, which it set; the sensor reading; and the clock. Before every run it had to write down a prediction and a confidence; the prediction was saved before the fan was touched, and it scored itself afterwards. When the world stopped matching the model it was not allowed to stop: it had to declare that the world had changed and rebuild.

In the first session (twenty runs) it followed the rules and found four edges of its world. Three of them were the instrument: electrical noise from the motor bleeding into the sensor, a reverse direction that was actually a dead wire, and an unconnected input picking up mains hum, which it modelled as a slow, real oscillation. I fixed the rig and told the agent only that the system had been serviced.

In the second session (thirty-two runs) it wrote an equation for where the arm settles for a given fan speed, y = 715 − 0.0118(x − 192)², predicted whole runs it had not been fitted on with a skill of 0.66–0.81 (1 is a perfect model, 0 is "nothing happens"), and stayed calibrated: across thirty-nine predictions its stated confidence and its hit rate differed by 0.03. Then, at run 031, the readings changed character. The agent did exactly what the rules asked: declared a new regime, cited the runs, rebuilt from its opening probes, tried five ways to get the old world back, and stopped by choice with the sentence that this was a state only a service, not a command, seemed to reset.

It was right about that and wrong about what it was. A phone camera had been filming the arm, synchronised to the board's LED, which switches with the fan. Tracking the propeller hub gave a second angle that owed nothing to the sensor. In one run the arm sat still at sixteen degrees while the sensor read anywhere between 333 and 737; in another the arm was still to a twentieth of a degree while the sensor cycled every seven seconds. The new regime was the potentiometer's wiper breaking contact under vibration. Everything the agent modelled cleanly was real; everything it called oscillation was the sensor. And the pendulum was never a pendulum: there is so much friction in a potentiometer's shaft that the arm moves to its new balance point in 0.4 seconds and stops dead.

Against the criteria written down before the second session: calibration passed, prediction skill passed on four held-out runs, the Brier score on yes/no claims failed the bar (0.33 against 0.20), no oscillation period was found because the rig has none, and fourteen of the thirty-two runs had to be excluded as instrument failures. A mixed result, reported as mixed. The model could not discover a category it was never given — "the sensor can lie" was not in its world — and only an independent witness separated instrument from world.

But real intelligence is never about modelling what is simple and known. It is about modelling what is messy and unknown.

It may perform the same experiment twice, but get two different results. The question is: can it determine the threshold between unexplainable error bars, or a new latent variable determining new rules of the world?

How does it know when the conditions of the world have changed? What thresholds does it hold for each changing state?

And how does it get quicker at determining the rules? Is there a cost between a high accuracy of the modelled world and the number of experiments it performs to model the world?

These are questions I hope to find the answers to someday — although, as with all things in life, it depends on context and domain.

More to come as I iterate further on this experiment and more.

Materials

Everything used, as used, rough by design: wind-pendulum-materials.zip (5 MB) — the harness the agent played through, the rules it was given and the sandbox that enforced them, the firmware, the camera tracker, and both sessions' records: every run as CSV, the prediction ledger, the agent's own model notes and analysis scripts, and the reviews. The agent was Claude Code; videos are not included.

On method: the rig is an Elegoo Mega kit and a kitchen skewer. The firmware, the harness the agent plays through, and the camera tracking were built in long collaboration with AI; the agent playing blind was Claude Code. The judgement calls and the responsibility sit with the author.
